{% capture clinical %}**Background**: Patients with tuberculosis (TB) face an elevated risk of certain cardiovascular diseases. The inflammatory response associated with TB is linked to cardiac complications, though the spatial impact on cardiac structure remains understudied. In this study, we assess population-level differences in cardiac morphology in TB using an atlas-based approach applied to routine chest X-rays. **Methods**: A total of 8,651 patients (2,626 with TB) were selected from four cohorts: Shenzhen, TBX11K, TB Portals, and MIMIC — covering seven countries. We developed a representative cardiac template, the CardioMorph Atlas, and compared population-level morphological differences between patients with TB and controls. Cardiac borders and areas were quantified into CardioMorph features and analysed for their association with all-cause mortality using Cox regression. **Findings**: CardioMorph Atlas revealed significant lateral elongation of the heart in patients with TB, in both left and right ventricles, with patterns distinct from those in pneumonia and cardiomegaly. Vector field analysis showed lateral extension of cardiac structures in patients with TB. Upper area (hazard ratio: 2.18, 95% CI: 1.10–4.30, p = 0.022) and right perimeter (HR = 2.07, 95% CI: 1.06–4.02) were significantly associated with increased all-cause mortality in patients with TB.
{% endcapture %}
{% capture technical %}Can a disease of the lungs leave a fingerprint on the heart's outline, visible on the same 2D projection radiograph already being taken for TB screening? We built the CardioMorph Atlas by segmenting cardiac chambers and lung fields on 8,651 chest X-rays across four cohorts spanning seven countries, registering every heart to a median-area reference template (rigid + affine, DICE 0.841), converting chamber masks to signed distance functions, and running voxel-wise, permutation-corrected GLM comparisons (FSL `randomise`, 500 permutations) between TB and no-finding controls. The atlas isn't a classifier — it's closer to a population-level deformation field, asking where the TB heart systematically diverges from the reference shape after correcting for thousands of simultaneous voxelwise tests on registered anatomy. The answer wasn't what classic cardiomegaly would predict: TB doesn't produce asymmetric, apex-directed enlargement, it produces a diffuse, bilateral, lateral widening of both ventricles, independently confirmed by a displacement vector field analysis that B-spline-aligns TB and control contours and averages the resulting vectors on a polar grid. We stress-tested TB-specificity by rerunning the identical pipeline on pneumonia, atelectasis, pneumothorax, and cardiomegaly in MIMIC: the first three gave only small, randomly-oriented displacement noise (no directional signal even where mediastinal shift is expected), cardiomegaly gave the classic left-apex-directed pattern, and TB's diffuse bilateral signature was distinct from all four. Collapsing the atlas to four landmark points per chamber gave compact CardioMorph border/area features (upper, anterior, inferior, left, right), which we then tested for prognostic value independent of clinical covariates. In the TB Portals cohort, a 6-month landmark Cox analysis — adjusted for age, sex, BMI, country, HIV status, drug resistance, Hepatitis C, diabetes, TB site, and lung localisation — showed increased upper area (HR 2.18) and upper perimeter (HR 2.07) predicted higher all-cause mortality, while decreased right perimeter predicted lower mortality. The most unexpected part isn't that shape correlates with outcome, it's that a signal this subtle, hiding in the silhouette of an organ TB doesn't directly infect, survives that much covariate adjustment on an imaging modality that's already sitting in every TB screening workflow on Earth.
